The Classical Case: Pontryagin–van Kampen Duality

Setup and dual group $\widehat{G}$

  • Let $G$ be a locally compact abelian (LCA) topological group.
  • Dual group: $\widehat{G} := \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{cont}}(G, \mathbb{T})$, also written $G^* = \widehat{G}$, where $\mathbb{T} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : \lvert z\rvert = 1\}$.
  • Equipped with pointwise multiplication and the compact-open topology, $\widehat{G}$ is again an LCA group.
Pontryagin–van Kampen Theorem
The canonical evaluation map, $\Phi\colon G \longrightarrow (G^*)^*$, defined by $$\Phi(g)(\chi) = \chi(g) \quad (\forall g \in G,\; \chi \in G^*),$$ is an isomorphism of topological groups ($G \cong (G^*)^*$).

Key Takeaway. When $G$ is abelian, $\widehat{G}$ is a group because the pointwise product of two characters is a character as well.

Behind Duality: Group Structure

Definition
A topological group $H$ is compactly generated if there is a compact subset $K \subseteq H$ such that $$\langle K\rangle = H.$$
Theorem
Every LCA group $G$ has an open subgroup $H$ which is compactly generated.

Duality in a Nutshell

$$\begin{array}{ccccccccc} 0 & \longrightarrow & H & \longrightarrow & G & \longrightarrow & G/H & \longrightarrow & 0 \\[4pt] & & \phantom{{\scriptstyle\Phi_H}}\Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\Phi_H} & & \phantom{{\scriptstyle\Phi_G}}\Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\Phi_G} & & \phantom{{\scriptstyle\Phi_{G/H}}}\Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\Phi_{G/H}} & & \\[4pt] 0 & \longrightarrow & H^{**} & \longrightarrow & G^{**} & \longrightarrow & (G/H)^{**} & \longrightarrow & 0 \end{array}$$

It is enough to prove duality for:

(i) compactly generated groups ($H$)
(ii) discrete groups ($G/H$)

Tools of the Trade

Theorem
Let $G$ be an LCA group. Then:
(i) $\widehat{G}$ separate the points of $G$: if $g_1 \neq g_2$, then there is $\chi \in \widehat{G}$ with $\chi(g_1) \neq \chi(g_2)$;
(ii) if in addition $G$ is compact, $\mathcal{A} = \operatorname{span}\,\widehat{G}$, also called the “trigonometric polynomials”, is uniformly dense in $C(G)$.
  • (i) ⇒ (ii): $\mathcal{A}$ contains the constants, is closed under products, and under conjugation since $\overline{\chi} = \chi^{-1} \in \widehat{G}$. With (i), the Stone–Weierstrass theorem gives density.

Why Duality Matters: Key Applications (1)

Unifying classical harmonic analysis

  • Fourier Transform: unifies Fourier series ($G = \mathbb{T}$), Fourier integrals ($G = \mathbb{R}^n$), and Discrete Fourier Transforms ($G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$) under one framework:
$$\mathcal{F}\colon L^1(G) \longrightarrow C_0(\widehat{G}), \qquad \widehat{f}(\chi) = \int_G f(g)\overline{\chi(g)}\,d\mu(g)$$
  • Structure Theory: translates hard topological properties of $G$ into dual properties of $\widehat{G}$ (e.g., $G$ is compact $\iff \widehat{G}$ is discrete).

Why Duality Matters: Key Applications (2)

Building block for non-abelian representation theory

  • Mackey’s Little Group Method: for semidirect products $G = N \rtimes K$ with $N$ abelian and the $K$-action on $\widehat{N}$ regular, the irreducible representations of $G$ are classified via $K$-orbits in the Pontryagin dual $\widehat{N}$, together with the irreducible representations of the stabilizers (little groups).
Example: Heisenberg group
$H_3(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^2 \rtimes \mathbb{R}$. Using $\widehat{\mathbb{R}^2} \cong \mathbb{R}^2$, orbit analysis yields all irreducible unitary representations.

Breaking Commutativity: When Pontryagin Duality Fails

Why 1-dimensional characters fail

  • Any continuous homomorphism $\chi\colon G \to \mathbb{T}$ must be trivial on the commutator subgroup $[G, G]$:
$$\chi(ghg^{-1}h^{-1}) = 1$$
  • Extreme failure: for simple non-abelian compact groups like $SU(2)$ or $SO(3)$, $\overline{[G,G]} = G$, so the only 1D character is trivial ($\widehat{G} = \{1\}$).
Conclusion
1D representations are blind to non-abelian structure; we must move to higher-dimensional unitary representations.

Group Representations

Definition (representation)
A representation of a locally compact group $G$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ is a group homomorphism $$\pi\colon G \to GL(\mathcal{H}) $$ that is strongly continuous ($g \mapsto \pi(g)\xi$ is continuous for every $\xi \in \mathcal{H}$). It is unitary if every $\pi(g)$ is a unitary operator. We denote $H_{\pi}=H$ for convenience.
Definition (irreducible representation)
A unitary representation $\pi$ of $G$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H} \neq \{0\}$ is irreducible if the only closed subspaces of $\mathcal{H}$ invariant under $\pi(G)$ are $\{0\}$ and $\mathcal{H}$.
  • For $G$ LCA, all irreducible unitary representations are 1D.

Equivalence of Representations

Definition (equivalence)
Two unitary representations $\pi_1\colon G \to U(\mathcal{H}_1)$ and $\pi_2\colon G \to U(\mathcal{H}_2)$ are equivalent, written $\pi_1 \cong \pi_2$, if there is a unitary operator $U\colon \mathcal{H}_1 \to \mathcal{H}_2$ such that $$U\pi_1(g) = \pi_2(g)U \quad \text{for all } g \in G.$$

Some things never change: Point Separation

Theorem (Gelfand–Raikov, 1943)
Let $G$ be any locally compact group. For every $g \neq e$ in $G$, there exists an irreducible unitary representation $\pi$ such that $$\pi(g) \neq I.$$
Takeaway
Even without commutativity, irreducible unitary representations separate the points of $G$: no group information is lost.

The Advantage of Compact Groups

  • Structure: we need not worry about structural issues, as in the general abelian case.For compact abelian $G$ there is no need to reduce to compactly generated subgroups.
  • Representations: the representation theory of compact groups is much easier.Every representation is unitarizable and splits into finite-dimensional irreducibles (next slide).
  • Haar measure: there is $\mu$, a unique Haar probability measure.Linked to a faithful state.

Representations of Compact Groups

Theorem (unitarity for compact groups)
Let $G$ be a compact group and $\pi$ a representation of $G$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. Then $\mathcal{H}$ has an equivalent inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle'$ for which $\pi$ is unitary; that is, $\pi$ is similar to a unitary representation.
Theorem (complete reducibility)
Let $\pi$ be a unitary representation of a compact group $G$ on $\mathcal{H}$. Then $$\mathcal{H} = \bigoplus_{i \in I} \mathcal{H}_i \quad \text{(orthogonal Hilbert sum)},$$ where each $\mathcal{H}_i$ is a closed, finite-dimensional, $\pi(G)$-invariant subspace on which $\pi$ restricts to an irreducible representation.

Dual group structure: Gone

  • Given two irreducible unitary representations $\rho, \sigma$ of a compact group $G$, their tensor product $(\rho \otimes \sigma)(g) = \rho(g) \otimes \sigma(g)$ is again a finite-dimensional unitary representation of $G$. But it might not be irreducible.
  • By complete reducibility, it decomposes back into irreducibles:
$$\rho \otimes \sigma \cong \bigoplus_{\pi} m_{\rho,\sigma}^{\pi}\, \pi$$ The sum runs over irreducible representations $\pi$ up to equivalence, with multiplicities $m_{\rho,\sigma}^{\pi} \in \mathbb{N}$, only finitely many nonzero.

Conjugate (Contragredient) Representations

Why conjugate representations are invisible in the abelian case

  • For a 1D character $\chi\colon G \to \mathbb{T}$, complex conjugation is just the group inverse:
$$\overline{\chi(g)} = \chi(g)^{-1} \quad \text{for all } g \in G, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \overline{\chi} = \chi^{-1} \in \widehat{G}.$$
  • Thus, conjugation requires no extra structure: it is already built into the group operation of $\widehat{G}$.

Conjugate Representations: The General Case

Definition (conjugate representation)
Let $\pi$ be a unitary representation of $G$ on $\mathcal{H}$, and $\overline{\mathcal{H}}$ the conjugate Hilbert space ($\mathcal{H}$ with scalar multiplication conjugated). The conjugate representation is $\overline{\pi}(g) := \pi(g)$ acting on $\overline{\mathcal{H}}$.
  • In an orthonormal basis, $\overline{\pi}(g)$ is the entrywise conjugate of the matrix $\pi(g)$.
  • $\pi$ is irreducible if and only if $\overline{\pi}$ is.
  • For non-abelian $G$, conjugation $\pi \mapsto \overline{\pi}$ is genuinely an extra structure on the dual.

Conjugation Is Genuinely New: An Example

  • Let $\pi\colon SU(3) \to U(3)$ be the representation $\pi(g) = g$. Then $\pi \not\cong \overline{\pi}$.
  • Take $g = e^{2\pi i/3} I \in SU(3)$, which has $\det g = e^{2\pi i} = 1$. The traces differ:
$$\operatorname{Tr}(\pi(g)) = 3e^{2\pi i/3} \neq 3e^{-2\pi i/3} = \operatorname{Tr}(\overline{\pi}(g))$$
  • Equivalent representations have the same character, since $\operatorname{Tr}(UAU^{-1}) = \operatorname{Tr}(A)$. Hence $\pi$ and $\overline{\pi}$ are not equivalent.

Intertwinings

Definition (intertwining operator)
Let $\rho, \sigma$ be representations of $G$ on Hilbert spaces $\mathcal{H}_\rho, \mathcal{H}_\sigma$. An intertwining operator between $\rho$ and $\sigma$ is a bounded linear operator $T\colon \mathcal{H}_\rho \to \mathcal{H}_\sigma$ such that $$T\rho(g) = \sigma(g)T \quad \text{for all } g \in G.$$
Theorem (averaging)
Let $G$ be a compact group with normalized Haar measure $\mu$, let $\rho, \sigma$ be unitary representations of $G$, and let $T\colon \mathcal{H}_\rho \to \mathcal{H}_\sigma$ be a bounded operator. Then $$\tilde{T} = \int_G \sigma(g)^*\,T\,\rho(g)\,d\mu(g)$$ is an intertwining operator between $\rho$ and $\sigma$.

Schur Orthogonality

Schur’s Lemma

  • Let $\pi, \rho$ be finite-dimensional unitary irreducible representations of a compact group $G$, and let $T\colon \mathcal{H}_\pi \to \mathcal{H}_\rho$ be an intertwining operator ($T\pi(g) = \rho(g)T$ for all $g \in G$):
  • If $\pi \not\cong \rho$, then $T = 0$.
  • If $\pi = \rho$, then $T = \lambda I$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$.

Orthogonality of matrix coefficients

  • Taking $T=E_{kq}:H_{\pi}\to H_{\rho}$, rank-one operator, produces an intertwiner, yielding:
$$\int_G v^{(\pi)}_{jk}(g)\,\overline{v^{(\rho)}_{p,q}(g)} d\mu(g) = \frac{1}{d_\pi}\,\delta_{\pi,\rho}\,\delta_{j,p}\,\delta_{k,q}$$ where, with respect to an orthonormal basis, $\pi(g)=[v_{jk}^{(\pi)}]$.

Axiomatizing the Dual: Krein Algebras (1)

Setup
A Krein algebra is a triple $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$, where $A$ is a unital, associative, commutative complex algebra with an involution $x \mapsto x^*$ (conjugate-linear, $(xy)^* = y^*x^*$, $(x^*)^* = x$), and $\mathcal{B}$ is a Hamel basis of $A$ satisfying the six axioms below.
  • Axiom 1 (Blocks). $\mathcal{B}$ is partitioned as $\{v^{(\alpha)}_{jk} : \alpha \in N,\ 1 \le j,k \le d_\alpha\}$; for each $\alpha$ we form the matrix $V^{(\alpha)} = [v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}]$.
  • Axiom 2 (Identity block). There is $0 \in N$ with $d_0 = 1$ and $V^{(0)} = [1]$.

Axiomatizing the Dual: Krein Algebras (2)

  • Axiom 3 (Conjugate blocks). For every $\alpha \in N$ there are $\bar{\alpha} \in N$ with $d_{\bar{\alpha}} = d_\alpha$ and a unitary matrix $U_\alpha \in U(d_\alpha)$ such that
$$\overline{V^{(\alpha)}} := \bigl[(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk})^*\bigr] = U_\alpha V^{(\bar{\alpha})} U_\alpha^{-1}.$$
  • Axiom 4 (Kronecker product decomposition). For all $\alpha, \beta \in N$, the Kronecker product $V^{(\alpha)} \otimes V^{(\beta)}$ is unitarily equivalent to a direct sum of basis blocks:
$$W_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(V^{(\alpha)} \otimes V^{(\beta)}\bigr)W_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} = \bigoplus_{\gamma \in N} m_{\alpha\beta}^\gamma\, V^{(\gamma)}.$$

Here $W_{\alpha\beta}$ is unitary, and the multiplicities $m_{\alpha\beta}^\gamma \in \mathbb{N}$ are nonzero for only finitely many $\gamma$.

Axiomatizing the Dual: Krein Algebras (3)

  • Axiom 5 (Trivial-block multiplicity). In the decomposition of Axiom 4, the identity block $V^{(0)}$ appears if and only if $V^{(\alpha)} \cong \overline{V^{(\beta)}}$, and in that case it appears exactly once.
  • Axiom 6 (Block unitarity). For every $\alpha \in N$, the matrix block $V^{(\alpha)}$ is unitary:
$$\sum_{r=1}^{d_\alpha} v^{(\alpha)}_{jr}\,(v^{(\alpha)}_{kr})^* = \delta_{jk}\,1 = \sum_{r=1}^{d_\alpha} (v^{(\alpha)}_{rj})^*\,v^{(\alpha)}_{rk}.$$

The Krein Algebra of a Compact Group (1)

  • Let $G$ be a compact topological group, and let $\widehat{G}$ denote a set of complete representatives of the unitary irreducible representations of $G$ up to equivalence.
  • For $\alpha \in \widehat{G}$, let $\pi_\alpha\colon G \to U(d_\alpha)$ be the corresponding representation, written in an orthonormal basis (irreducible unitary representations of a compact group are finite-dimensional).
Matrix coefficients
$$v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}(g) := \bigl(\pi_\alpha(g)\bigr)_{jk}, \qquad 1 \le j,k \le d_\alpha, \quad g \in G.$$

The Krein Algebra of a Compact Group (2)

Definition
Take $N = \widehat{G}$, $\mathcal{B}_G = \{v^{(\alpha)}_{jk} : \alpha \in N,\ 1 \le j,k \le d_\alpha,\alpha \in N\}$ (matrix coefficients of $\pi_\alpha \in \alpha$ with respect to an orthonormal basis) and denote $$\mathcal{E}(G) := \operatorname{span}\,\mathcal{B}_G \subseteq C(G),$$ with pointwise operations and involution $f^*(g) = \overline{f(g)}$.

Then $(\mathcal{E}(G), \mathcal{B}_G, \hat{G})$ is a Krein algebra.

  • The $v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}$ are linearly independent by Schur orthogonality, so $\mathcal{B}_G$ is a Hamel basis. The other axioms come from the trivial representation, conjugate and tensor product representations, and unitarity of each $\pi_\alpha(g)$.

An example: Krein algbebra of $SU(2)$

Theorem
There is a family of unitary irreps, $\{(\pi_n,U(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}))\}_{n=1}^\infty$, such that if $(\pi,H_\pi)$ is an unitary irrep then $\exists!$ $p\in \mathbb{N}$ with $d_\pi=p+1$ and $\pi\cong \pi_p$. In particular $\overline{\pi_n}\cong \pi_n$, for all $n$.
Theorem (Clebsch–Gordan decomposition)
With the notation given above, for $m\geq n \geq 1$: $\pi_m\otimes \pi_n\cong \oplus_{k=0}^n \pi_{m+n-2k}$
  • Lets $[n]$ or $[\pi_n]$ denote the equivalence class of $\pi_n$.
  • We think $\{[n]:n\in \mathbb{N}\}$ as a set of generator with operations: \begin{eqnarray*} [n]\oplus [m]&=&[\pi_n\oplus \pi_m], \\ \text{if $m\geq n$:}\quad [n]\otimes[m]=[m]\otimes[n]&=&\oplus_{k=0}^n [n+m-2k] \end{eqnarray*}

Reconstructing the Group from a Krein Algebra

Structure space
Let $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ be a Krein algebra. Its structure space (the group of $*$-characters) is $$\mathcal{G}(A) := \bigl\{\omega \in \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{alg}}(A, \mathbb{C}) \setminus \{0\} : \omega(x^*) = \overline{\omega(x)} \;\; \forall x \in A\bigr\}.$$
Group operation (block-matrix multiplication)
For $\omega_1, \omega_2 \in \mathcal{G}(A)$, define $\omega_1 \cdot \omega_2$ on the basis $\mathcal{B}$ by $$(\omega_1 \cdot \omega_2)\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr) := \sum_{r=1}^{d_\alpha} \omega_1\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jr}\bigr)\,\omega_2\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{rk}\bigr).$$

NC point of view

  • A nonzero, $*$-preserving linear map $\omega\colon A \to \mathbb{C}$ lies in $\mathcal{G}(A)$ if and only if $$\omega\bigl(V^{(\alpha)} \otimes V^{(\beta)}\bigr) = \omega\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr) \otimes \omega\bigl(V^{(\beta)}\bigr)$$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in N$ ($\omega$ applied entrywise).
  • Equivalently, group multiplication is blockwise: for all $\alpha \in N$, $$(\omega_1 \cdot \omega_2)\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr) = \omega_1\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr)\,\omega_2\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr).$$
Identity and inverse
Identity: $\varepsilon\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr) = I_{d_\alpha}$, that is, $\varepsilon\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr) = \delta_{jk}$ for all $\alpha$.
Inverse: $\omega^{-1}\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr) = \omega\bigl(V^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{-1}$, that is, $\omega^{-1}\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr) = \overline{\omega\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{kj}\bigr)}$.

Reconstructing the topology

Topology
Equip $\mathcal{G}(A)$ with the weak-$*$ topology (pointwise convergence on $A$). By Tychonoff’s theorem and block unitarity, $\mathcal{G}(A)$ is compact Hausdorff.
Theorem
$\mathcal{G}(A)$ is a compact Hausdorff topological group.

Tannaka–Krein Duality Theorem (1)

Theorem 1 (Group reconstruction; Tannaka, 1938)
Let $G$ be any compact group and $\mathcal{E}(G)$ its Krein algebra. The evaluation map $\Phi\colon G \longrightarrow \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}(G))$, defined by $\Phi(g)(f) = f(g)$, is an isomorphism of topological groups: $$G \cong \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}(G)).$$
  • A compact group is recovered from the algebra $\mathcal{E}(G)$ of its matrix coefficients.

Sketch of the Proof

The secret ingredient: Positivity

Let $\eta\colon \mathcal{E}(G) \to \mathbb{C}$ be a linear functional. The following are equivalent:

  1. $\eta\bigl(\lvert f\rvert^2\bigr) \geq 0$ for all $f \in \mathcal{E}(G)$;
  2. $\eta(h) \geq 0$ for all $h \in \mathcal{E}^+(G)$.
Each of these implies $$\lvert\eta(f)\rvert \leq \eta(1)\,\lVert f\rVert_\infty \qquad (f \in \mathcal{E}(G)).$$ Here $\mathcal{E}^+(G) = \{h \in \mathcal{E}(G) : h \geq 0\}$.

Sketch of the Proof (2)

Surjectivity

Let $\omega\colon \mathcal{E}(G) \to \mathbb{C}$ be a nonzero $*$-algebra homomorphism (that is, $\omega \in \mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}(G))$). Then

$$\omega\bigl(\lvert f\rvert^2\bigr) = \omega(f)\,\omega(\overline{f}) = \lvert\omega(f)\rvert^2 \geq 0.$$

By the previous slide, $\lvert\omega(f)\rvert \leq \omega(1)\,\lVert f\rVert_\infty = \lVert f\rVert_\infty$, so the linear and multiplicative map $\omega$ is continuous for the uniform norm. Hence it extends to $\tilde{\omega}\colon \overline{\mathcal{E}(G)} \to \mathbb{C}$, where the closure is taken in the uniform norm.

Sketch of the Proof (3)

Surjectivity (continued)

  • But $\mathcal{E}(G)$ is an algebra, contains the constants, is closed under conjugation and separates the points of $G$. Hence $\overline{\mathcal{E}(G)} = C(G)$.
  • In conclusion, $\tilde{\omega}\colon C(G) \to \mathbb{C}$ is a linear, multiplicative, nonzero map. Hence there is $g \in G$ such that $$\tilde{\omega}(f) = f(g) \quad \text{for all } f \in C(G).$$
  • In particular $\omega = \Phi(g)$, so $\Phi$ is surjective.

Tannaka–Krein Duality Theorem (2)

Theorem 2 (Algebra characterization; Krein, 1949)
Let $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ be any abstract Krein algebra, and set $G := \mathcal{G}(A)$. Then the map $\Psi\colon A \longrightarrow \mathcal{E}(G)$ sending $x \mapsto \widehat{x}$, where $\widehat{x}(\omega) = \omega(x)$, is an isomorphism of Krein algebras: $$(A, \mathcal{B}, N) \cong \bigl(\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G}(A)),\, \mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{G}(A)},\, \widehat{\mathcal{G}(A)}\bigr).$$
  • Every abstract Krein algebra is the Krein algebra of a compact group, namely $\mathcal{G}(A)$.

Revisiting the Axioms

  1. Basis given in blocks: $\mathcal{B} = \{V^{(\alpha)}\}_{\alpha \in N}$.
  2. Trivial block: $V^{(0)} = [1]$.
  3. Conjugates: up to unitary equivalence, closed under conjugation.
  4. Tensor products: up to unitary equivalence, closed under tensor products.
  5. Trivial block multiplicity.
  6. Unitary blocks.
The less clear: Trivial block multiplicity.
This axiom hides positivity and the Haar measure. After all, if Tannaka’s theorem recovers $G$ from its Krein algebra $\mathcal{E}(G)$, how is the probability Haar measure $\mu$ represented in an abstract Krein algebra?

Haar Functional

  • Let $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ be a Krein algebra, with $\mathcal{B} = \{V^{(\alpha)}\}_{\alpha \in N}$ and $V^{(\alpha)} = \bigl[v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr]$, $1 \le j,k \le d_\alpha$.
Definition (Haar functional)
The Haar functional of $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ is the linear map $h\colon A \to \mathbb{C}$ such that $$h\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \alpha = 0,\\ 0 & \text{if } \alpha \neq 0. \end{cases}$$
  • For $A = \mathcal{E}(G)$, the Haar functional is integration against the Haar probability measure, $h(f) = \int_G f\,d\mu$, because $\int_G v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\,d\mu = 0$ for $\alpha \neq 0$ by Schur orthogonality.

From a humble Functional to an important State

Lemma
The Haar functional is a state: $h(1) = 1$ and $h(aa^*) \geq 0 \qquad \text{for all } a \in A.$
Even more
$$h\Bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\,\bigl(v^{(\beta)}_{pq}\bigr)^*\Bigr) = \frac{1}{d_\alpha}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta_{jp}\,\delta_{kq}.$$

Equivalent Definition

Assumptions
Let $A$ be a unital commutative $*$-algebra with a Hamel basis given by blocks $\mathcal{B} = \{V^{(\alpha)}\}_{\alpha \in N}$ that satisfy Axioms 1–6, except Axiom 5. Let $h\colon A \to \mathbb{C}$ denote the linear map given by $$h\bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\bigr) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \alpha = 0,\\ 0 & \text{if } \alpha \neq 0. \end{cases}$$
Theorem (Rodríguez-T)
If in addition $h$ is a state and satisfies $$h\Bigl(v^{(\alpha)}_{jk}\,\bigl(v^{(\beta)}_{pq}\bigr)^*\Bigr) = \frac{1}{d_\alpha}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta_{jp}\,\delta_{kq},$$ then $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ satisfies Axiom 5. Hence $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ is a Krein algebra.

Products of Krein Algebras (1)

Definition (product)
Let $(A_1, \mathcal{B}_1, N_1)$ and $(A_2, \mathcal{B}_2, N_2)$ be two Krein algebras with Haar functionals $h_1$ and $h_2$. We defined their product as $$\begin{gathered}(A_1, \mathcal{B}_1, N_1) \times (A_2, \mathcal{B}_2, N_2) \\ := (A_1 \otimes A_2,\ \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2,\ N_1 \times N_2),\end{gathered}$$ where $\mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2 = \bigl\{V^{(\alpha_1)} \otimes V^{(\alpha_2)}\bigr\}_{(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) \in N_1 \times N_2}$.

Products of Krein Algebras (2)

Theorem (Rodríguez-T)
$(A_1 \otimes A_2, \mathcal{B}_1 \otimes \mathcal{B}_2, N_1 \times N_2)$ is a Krein algebra with Haar functional $h_1 \otimes h_2$.
In consequence
$$\begin{gathered}(\mathcal{E}(G_1), \mathcal{B}_{G_1}, \widehat{G}_1) \times (\mathcal{E}(G_2), \mathcal{B}_{G_2}, \widehat{G}_2) \\ \cong (\mathcal{E}(G_1 \times G_2),\ \mathcal{B}_{G_1 \times G_2},\ \widehat{G_1 \times G_2}).\end{gathered}$$ This is a non-commutative analogue of the fact that for LCA groups, $$\widehat{G_1 \times G_2} \cong \widehat{G}_1 \times \widehat{G}_2.$$

NC sets and Krein Algebras

Let $\tilde{A}$ be a unital commutative $*$-algebra. Assume there are:

  1. a set of indices $N$ and, for each $\nu \in N$, a natural number $d_\nu \in \mathbb{N}$ and a set $\Omega_\nu \subset U_{d_\nu}(\tilde{A})$;
  2. $A$ is the $*$-subalgebra of $\tilde{A}$ generated by the entries of the matrices in $\Omega_\nu$, $\nu \in N$.

NC multiplicative sets

Suppose the following properties hold:

  1. There is a unique $0 \in N$ such that $d_0 = 1$ and $\Omega_0 = \{[1]\}$.
  2. For each $\nu \in N$ and $S \in U_{d_\nu}(\mathbb{C})$, we have $S^*\Omega_\nu S = \Omega_\nu$.
  3. For each $\nu \in N$ there is $\bar{\nu} \in N$ such that $\overline{\Omega_\nu} \subseteq \Omega_{\bar{\nu}}$ (entrywise conjugate matrices).
  4. For each $V_1 \in \Omega_{\mu_1}$ and $V_2 \in \Omega_{\mu_2}$, there are $\nu_1, \dots, \nu_k \in N$ and $W_i \in \Omega_{\nu_i}$ such that $V_1 \otimes V_2 \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^k W_i$.
  5. For $V \in \Omega_{\mu}$ and $V' \in \Omega_{\mu'}$, $V$ and $V'$ are unitarily equivalent if and only if $\mu = \mu'$.

A Construction of Krein Algebras

In addition, assume there is a linear functional $h\colon A \to \mathbb{C}$ such that

  • $\, h(1) = 1$;
  • $\, h(V_{jk}) = 0$ for all $V \in \Omega_\nu$, $\nu \in N \setminus \{0\}$.

Conclusion
Fix matrices $V^{(\nu)} \in \Omega_\nu$ and let $\mathcal{B} = \{V^{(\nu)}\}_{\nu \in N}$. Then $(A, \mathcal{B}, N)$ is a Krein algebra.
